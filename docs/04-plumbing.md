# 04 Plumbing

There are no fittings in the liquid path. The pump's own silicone tubing runs unbroken from the jug to the drain line. Every joint you do not have is a joint that cannot leak.

## The cleanout cap

Most condensate drain lines have a vertical riser with a cap on top, which is where you would normally pour vinegar. Many are friction fit rather than glued.

**Buy a spare cap and drill that one.** They cost a couple of dollars and it keeps your original as a fallback.

1. Identify your cap size. 3/4 inch Schedule 40 is common; the part number is usually moulded into it.
2. Drill a **3/16 inch hole** in the top of the spare cap. That is 4.76 mm against 5 mm tubing, which gives a press fit that grips.
3. Push the tubing through. Warm or wet the end and twist it. If you drill 5 mm it will slide out over time.
4. Cut the tubing so it hangs about **3 inches into the riser**, ending in open air.
5. Fit a zip tie on the tubing just above the cap as a mechanical stop, so it cannot creep down over the years.

**The tube end must never sit in standing water.** If it does, you give condensate a path to wick back up.

## Why not a fitting

Earlier iterations of this build used a threaded stack: a glued female adapter, a reducing bushing and a barbed fitting. It works but adds three leak paths, several inches of height, and a corrosion question. Vinegar attacks brass, and stainless grade matters above 50C.

A hole and a press fit has none of those problems and costs nothing.

## The jug

Use the jug the vinegar came in. HDPE, rigid, already the right shape. Drill two holes in its cap.

**Pickup hole:** 3/16 inch, press fit for the tubing. Cut the tube so it ends about 5 mm off the bottom of the jug. Close enough to draw nearly all of it, far enough that it cannot seal against the floor.

**Vent hole:** about 3 mm. This one must stay in the **air space above the liquid**, never below it.

The vent exists because as the pump draws liquid out, air has to get back in or the jug collapses and the pump starves. Attic heat expanding the air above the vinegar is the second reason.

**A vent tube must not reach into the liquid.** If it does, you have built a siphon and the jug will empty itself.

If you fit a short stub of tube in the vent hole, run it outside the jug, loop it downward, and end it inside the containment bucket. Attic heat can push vapour or a little liquid out, and the loop catches it.

## Containment

**This is a requirement, not a suggestion.**

The jug sits inside a bucket whose capacity exceeds the jug's. A 1 gallon jug in a 2 gallon bucket gives you a full jug failure fully contained.

Put a **water leak sensor in the bottom of the bucket**, beside the jug. Containment without detection means you find out at the next refill.

## Layout

Two rules:

**The jug sits lower than the injection point.** The pump self primes easily over that height, and it means no head pressure is trying to push liquid toward the drain line if anything fails.

**Nothing pulls on the jug.** Route the tubing so no tug can tip it inside the bucket.

## Which tube is which

Run the pump and feel both ends. One will be under suction, and that is the inlet: it goes into the jug. The other goes to the cap.

A peristaltic pump is a brushed DC motor, so reversing the leads reverses the flow direction. If your inlet and outlet are the wrong way round, swap the two pump wires.

Mark both tubes as soon as you know.
